Your first Read-write session: a routine, not a leap of faith

Lessons 1 and 2 gave you the theory: Read-write is safe because every change goes through FH's own undo, and a bad answer is usually a scripting slip you can ask Claude to explain. This lesson turns that into a routine — four small steps, every time, until it's muscle memory and you don't need the checklist any more.

The routine

  1. Back up first. Same discipline you'd already use before any manual editing session in FH — this isn't a Bridge-specific step, it's your existing habit, applied here too. Read-only sessions don't need this; skip it there.
  2. State the edit precisely. Not "tidy up John's dates" — say exactly what you want changed and to what: "set John Smith's birth date to 12 Mar 1841, per the 1841 census." A precise instruction is also easier to verify afterwards.
  3. Watch it happen, then look yourself. The edit happens live in FH while the Session's running — the record you asked about is right there. Open it and confirm what actually changed, don't just take Claude's word for its own summary.
  4. Test the escape hatch once, on purpose. Press Ctrl-Z and confirm the edit reverts, then redo it (Ctrl-Y / Ctrl-Shift-Z) if you're keeping it. The first time you do this deliberately, on an edit you don't care about either way, is a better time to learn it than the first time you actually need it under pressure.
One more thing working in your favour

If a write script hits an error partway through, FH's auto-undo reverts it on its own — the project's error-handling deliberately lets that error surface rather than quietly swallowing it, precisely so this safety net gets the chance to fire. You don't have to trigger this yourself or even notice it happening; it's there whether or not the routine above catches something first.

What "state the edit precisely" buys you

You already know from Lesson 2 that a vague research question gets a clarifying question back rather than a guess. The same is true for a write instruction — but the stakes are higher, so don't rely on Claude to catch every ambiguity for you. "Fix his death date" invites Claude to decide what "fix" means; "set his death date to 4 Jul 1916, the CWGC-recorded date" doesn't. Precision here is doing double duty: it's both a good research practice you already have, and your first line of defence against an unwanted edit.

Quick check

Why does the routine have you press Ctrl-Z on purpose, even on an edit you're happy with?

Try it now

  1. Back up your project file.
  2. Start a Read-write Session.
  3. Ask Claude to make one precise, low-stakes edit — something you can undo without a second thought.
  4. Open the record in FH and confirm the change yourself, then Ctrl-Z it, then redo it.
